 How to write a strong personal statement for LSE 

 0 comments
 | 7 shares

Estimated reading time: 10 minutes

 7
 Shares

Your personal statement is one of the most important parts of your UCAS application to LSE. The School doesn’t interview for undergraduate places, so this is your only chance to show why you’re a good fit for the programme. No pressure! Read on for advice on how to write a strong personal statement for LSE.

Know the new format

The personal statement is structured around three questions rather than a single open essay. Each question has a minimum character count of 350, and the overall limit across all three is 4,000 characters. That’s not a lot of space, so every sentence needs to earn its place.

How to answer the three personal statement questions in your UCAS application

Do your research before you write

Before you start drafting, spend time on LSE’s programme pages. Research the programme you’ll be applying to carefully and understand what’s actually taught. Admissions selectors are looking for a clear alignment between your academic interests and the specific programme at LSE.

This matters more than you might think. If you’re applying for International History, for example, your statement should reflect an interest in modern international history (which is what LSE teaches), not ancient history. If you’re applying for a combined programme like BSc Politics and Economics, give equal weight to both subjects. A statement that leans heavily towards one side will be less competitive than a statement that explores why the interconnection between the two subjects appeals to you.

If you’re applying to slightly different programmes across your five UCAS choices, focus your statement on the areas of overlap so it works for all of them. LSE does not accept replacement or supplementary personal statements, so what you submit through UCAS is what they’ll read.

At least 80 per cent of your personal statement should focus on your academic interest in the subject. This is the heart of your application.

Question one: Why do you want to study this course or subject?

Think about what drew you to the subject, which aspects you find most interesting, and what the big issues are in the field. If there’s a specific area you want to focus on, say so. The key here is depth, not breadth. Selectors want to see that you’ve thought seriously about the subject, not just that you’ve listed topics you’ve come across.

One important thing to avoid: don’t talk about future career plans in this section. Your statement should reflect your academic interests, not what job you want after graduating.

Question two: How have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare for this course or subject?

This is where you show your wider engagement with the subject beyond the classroom. LSE is a research-intensive university, and selectors want to see evidence that you’ve explored the subject independently and reflected on what you’ve encountered.

You could talk about your super-curricular activities, i.e., academic exploration beyond your school syllabus. These include, but are not limited to, additional reading, podcasts, lectures, an EPQ, or a widening participation programme. But the emphasis should be on what you thought about what you engaged with, not just a list of what you read. If you attended a lecture or read a paper, what struck you? How did it deepen your understanding? What questions did it raise? What did you disagree with?

If you’re not sure where to start, listen to podcasts of LSE public events, look at suggested reading on the programme pages, or explore the published research of academics who teach on the course. These are all freely available and show great initiative. You can find out more about LSE’s public events in Josephine’s blog.

One thing LSE makes clear: selectors look for students who can best reflect on the opportunities available to them, not those who have had the best opportunities. So don’t worry if your experience looks different from someone else’s. What matters is what you did with it, what transferable skills you gained and how those skills will make you a good candidate at the university and your chosen course.

Question 3 – What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful?

This section covers experiences outside of formal education. If you’ve done relevant work experience, think about how it gave you a wider understanding of the topics you’ll study. If you’re applying for deferred entry, briefly mention your gap year plans. If you’re a post-qualified applicant, explain what you’ve been doing since your exams.

You can also use this space to expand on things you couldn’t fit into your answers to questions one and two. Writing the minimum of 350 characters here is perfectly acceptable, so don’t pad it out for the sake of length.

What to avoid

Don’t waste space listing your subjects. Your qualifications are already shown elsewhere on your UCAS form. Don’t name specific universities in your statement, as this won’t go down well with your other choices. And don’t spend more time on extracurriculars than on your subject. Reflection on transferable skills is fine, but your academic engagement should always take up the majority of the space.

Undergraduate programme applications: how to excel at them

A note on AI

LSE expects your personal statement to be entirely your own original work and reserves the right to reject applications where the statement has been created using AI tools. That said, AI can be used constructively to generate initial ideas, suggest reading materials (which you should cross-check with a teacher), or proofread your own writing. Just don’t let it write the statement for you.

My final advice

Start early. Write multiple drafts. Read your statement out loud to check it flows. Ask a teacher, a friend or reach out to current students or graduates on LinkedIn for feedback. And above all, focus on showing intellectual curiosity about your subject. That’s what LSE is looking for.

For the full guidance on writing your personal statement, visit LSE’s personal statement page.
