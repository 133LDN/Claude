UCAS Personal Statement 2027: The Three Questions

What the three UCAS questions ask, what's changed for 2027 entry, and how to answer each one well
Book a Free Consultation

By Leading Tuition Team | Published July 2026

If your child is starting Year 13 for the 2026–27 academic year and preparing a UCAS application for 2027 entry, the good news is that the personal statement format is not changing again. UCAS replaced the old single free-form essay with three structured questions for 2026 entry, and that same three-question format carries over unchanged into the 2027 cycle. What has changed is everything around it: the specific 2027 deadlines, a full year of real applicant experience with the new questions, and clearer guidance from admissions departments on what a strong answer actually looks like now that the format is no longer brand new. This guide sets out the exact, current question wording, the 2027-cycle dates that matter, and what the first full year of the new format has taught applicants and advisers.

Is the UCAS Personal Statement Format Changing for 2027 Entry?

No. UCAS's own guidance is published under the heading "writing your personal statement for 2026 entry onwards" — deliberately worded to signal that the three-question structure is the new standing format, not a one-year experiment. For 2027 entry, applicants answer the same three questions, within the same combined character budget, as the 2026 cohort did. There is no fourth question about career plans, despite that being one of the most searched questions among applicants once the 2026 cycle began — UCAS has confirmed the personal statement remains three questions only.

That stability is useful, because it means the guidance and worked examples that admissions specialists refined over the first full cycle now under this format are directly transferable to 2027 applicants, rather than needing to be reinvented. What does need updating each year is the practical detail: the exact dates in the 2027 cycle, and the specific lessons that have emerged now that university admissions tutors have read a full year's worth of three-question statements rather than none.

The Three Questions: Exact Current Wording

One of the most persistent sources of confusion in the first 2026 cycle was applicants and even some guides using paraphrased or slightly different versions of the three questions. The exact wording, confirmed directly from UCAS's own personal statement guidance, is as follows.

Question 1: "Why do you want to study this course or subject?"

Question 2: "How have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare for this course or subject?"

Question 3: "What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful?"

The precise phrasing of Question 2 is worth pausing on. It does not simply ask "how have you prepared" in general terms — it specifically asks how your qualifications and studies have prepared you. That wording is a deliberate signal: Question 2 is the box for academic and curriculum-linked preparation — your A-levels, IB subjects, EPQ, relevant coursework, and directly related super-curricular reading or projects. Question 3 is where activity outside of formal education belongs. Several first-cycle applicants blurred this line, using Question 2 as a general "here is everything I have done" answer and leaving Question 3 thin as a result. Reading the actual question wording carefully, rather than a paraphrase of it, avoids that mistake from the outset.

UCAS sets one combined character budget rather than three separate boxes with fixed sizes: 4,000 characters in total (including spaces and line breaks), with a required minimum of 350 characters for each individual answer. The questions themselves do not count towards this total — the 4,000 characters are entirely for your own writing. This has not changed since the format launched, and there is no indication from UCAS that it will change for 2027 entry.

How to Answer Question 1: Why Do You Want to Study This Course or Subject?

Question 1 asks for a reason, not a résumé. Admissions tutors reading thousands of these answers can spot, within a sentence or two, whether a student is describing genuine intellectual engagement or reciting what their A-level specification already covers. "I have always been fascinated by History and enjoyed studying the Cold War at A-level" tells an admissions tutor nothing they cannot already see on the UCAS form. A strong Question 1 instead names a specific book, article, documentary, or idea, and explains what it made the applicant think or question.

For example, a History applicant might describe how reading Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers changed how they thought about the causes of the First World War — not as a chain of inevitable decisions but as a series of contingent, almost accidental choices — and how that reframing made them want to study the historiographical debate itself at degree level. A Computer Science applicant might describe a specific unsolved problem they encountered while building a personal coding project, rather than a general love of "how computers work."

What to avoid: the clichés admissions tutors say they see hundreds of times a cycle — "I have been passionate about this subject since I was young," "this subject affects every part of daily life," "I want to make a difference." None of these are false, but none of them distinguish one applicant from another. Specificity is the only reliable differentiator in a 350–2,000 character answer.

How to Answer Question 2: How Have Your Qualifications and Studies Prepared You?

Because the official wording explicitly names "qualifications and studies," the strongest Question 2 answers draw a direct line from something the applicant has formally studied to a specific skill or insight relevant to the course. This is different from a general account of extracurricular enrichment — that belongs in Question 3.

Relevant content for Question 2 includes: A-level or IB coursework and how it connects to the degree, an Extended Project Qualification (EPQ) or IB Extended Essay on a related topic, participation in recognised subject competitions (the UK Mathematics Trust challenges, the Biology or Chemistry Olympiad, the British Informatics Olympiad for Computer Science applicants), completion of a relevant MOOC or online course, and directly subject-linked wider reading — provided the applicant explains what they took from it rather than simply naming the title.

For a Modern Languages applicant, Question 2 might connect A-level essay-writing skills in the target language to independent reading of a novel in the original language, explaining a specific translation or stylistic choice that stood out. For a Natural Sciences applicant at Cambridge, it might connect A-level Chemistry practical work to an independent investigation into reaction kinetics, explained with enough technical detail to show genuine engagement rather than a name-check.

The discipline to maintain here is relevance to formal study. If an activity did not arise from, or connect back to, a qualification or piece of coursework, it more naturally belongs in Question 3.

How to Answer Question 3: What Else Have You Done to Prepare, and Why Is It Relevant?

Question 3 is the shortest of the three answers in most sensible allocations, which means selectivity matters more here than anywhere else in the statement. The question has two parts, and both must be answered: what you did, and why it is useful for this specific course. An answer that lists activities without explaining their relevance — "I play the violin, volunteer at a charity shop, and captain my school's netball team" — fails the second half of the question entirely.

A stronger approach picks two, or at most three, experiences and explains the connection explicitly. Work experience shadowing in a clinical setting is not valuable because it happened; it is valuable because of what it revealed about clinical judgement under uncertainty, or about how a multidisciplinary team communicates. A part-time job is not valuable simply as evidence of "responsibility"; it is valuable if it taught something specific and relevant — handling a complaint calmly, managing competing priorities, explaining a technical process to a non-expert.

Caring responsibilities, sport captaincy, a Duke of Edinburgh expedition, a part-time job, or sustained volunteering can all work well here, provided the applicant is explicit about what the experience demonstrated and why an admissions tutor for this specific course should care.

What We Learned From the First Full Cycle Under This Format

The 2026 entry cycle was the first time any UCAS applicant answered these three questions rather than writing the old single essay, which means this is the first year it is possible to look back with any real evidence of how the format actually played out. Several patterns have become clear enough that 2027 applicants can plan around them directly, rather than guessing at how the new format would be read.

The "fourth question" myth persisted all cycle. A significant number of applicants and even some guidance sites searched for or assumed a fourth question about career plans or future ambitions would appear somewhere in the form. It did not, and UCAS has been explicit that the personal statement remains three questions only for 2027 entry as well. Do not draft a fourth answer and try to squeeze it in elsewhere.

Confusing Question 2 and Question 3 was the single most common structural error. Because Question 2's wording specifically names "qualifications and studies," answers that strayed into general extracurricular territory were read as under-answering the question as actually asked, not simply as a stylistic choice. Keep Question 2 anchored to formal study; keep broader activity in Question 3.

Universities have since published concrete character-split guidance where none existed before. In the early months of the format, admissions departments largely repeated UCAS's own minimums without offering a recommended split. A year on, institutions including LSE have published specific proportional guidance (for example, roughly 80% of the total character count across Questions 1 and 2 combined). Applicants drafting for 2027 can use this institution-level detail directly, which simply was not available to the first cohort.

Treating the three answers as one essay chopped into pieces continued to weaken statements. Advisers report that some of the flattest-reading statements in the first cycle were ones where a student had clearly drafted a single continuous piece of writing and then split it at three arbitrary points to fit the boxes. The three questions ask genuinely different things; each answer reads more convincingly when planned and drafted separately, then checked together for a consistent overall narrative.

The live character counter, not a word processor, remains the only reliable length check. Because UCAS counts characters including spaces, and the form displays a running total across all three boxes as you type, drafting in a word processor and copying across at the end routinely produces a mismatch. Draft directly in, or regularly check against, the UCAS form itself.

Subject-Specific Frameworks for the Three Questions

The three questions are identical for every applicant, but what counts as a strong answer differs sharply by subject and by the admissions test a course requires. Below are frameworks for four commonly applied-for subjects, chosen to cover a different mix of admissions routes than a general treatment of the format usually addresses.

Computer Science

Question 1: Anchor the answer in a specific technical problem the applicant tried to solve independently — an algorithmic inefficiency in a personal coding project, a specific limitation encountered while building something — rather than a general claim of loving "how computers work." Applicants to Cambridge, Warwick, Durham, and several other universities requiring the TMUA (Test of Mathematics for University Admission) should show the same instinct for structured mathematical reasoning that the test itself assesses.

Question 2: A-level Maths and Further Maths coursework, a personal programming project connected to a specific technical concept, participation in the British Informatics Olympiad, or an EPQ involving an algorithm or data structure all belong here, framed around the specific skill gained rather than the tools used.

Question 3: A hackathon, a work placement at a technology company, contributing to an open-source project, or running a small technical side-project for a club or society are strong choices, provided the reflection explains what the experience taught about working in the field rather than simply that it happened.

Natural Sciences (Cambridge)

Question 1: A specific scientific question or paper that the applicant found genuinely puzzling — not a general love of science across all disciplines — works far better as an opening. Applicants should be aware that Cambridge Natural Sciences, across its physical and biological routes, requires the ESAT (Engineering and Science Admissions Test), and showing the same instinct for applying scientific principles to an unfamiliar problem strengthens the answer.

Question 2: A-level Chemistry, Biology, or Physics coursework connected to an independent investigation, an EPQ with a laboratory or data-analysis component, or a relevant summer school belongs here, with the specific technical skill named explicitly (data interpretation, experimental design, statistical analysis).

Question 3: A research placement, a science fair project, or volunteering in a laboratory or science outreach setting are strong choices, tied to a specific reflection on what the experience revealed about how scientific research actually works day to day.

Modern Languages

Question 1: A specific text, film, or cultural moment encountered in the target language — and what it revealed about the language, culture, or a particular translation choice — is far stronger than a general statement about "loving languages." Oxford and Cambridge Modern Languages applicants, who sit the MLAT (Modern Languages Admissions Test), benefit from showing close attention to how language actually works, not just fluency.

Question 2: A-level or IB coursework in the target language, extended independent reading of a novel or play in the original language, or a linguistics-focused EPQ all belong here, explained through a specific stylistic or grammatical observation rather than a simple reading list.

Question 3: A period of study or work abroad, a language exchange, or tutoring younger students in the language are strong, specific choices, each tied to what the experience revealed about using the language in a real, unscripted setting.

History

Question 1: A specific historiographical debate or primary source that shifted the applicant's thinking — rather than an interest in "the past" in general — is a far stronger opening. Oxford History applicants, who sit the HAT (History Admissions Test), benefit from showing the same close, source-based analytical instinct that the test itself measures.

Question 2: A-level or IB coursework connected to an independent essay or EPQ on a related historical question, or wider reading of a specific historian's argument, belongs here, explained in terms of how it changed or refined the applicant's own analysis rather than simply summarised.

Question 3: Visiting an archive, volunteering at a museum or heritage site, or a school history society role are strong choices, provided the reflection connects the experience to a specific historical skill — handling primary evidence, public engagement with history, or research methodology.

Before and After: Making a Generic Answer Specific

Across every subject and every question, the difference between a weak and a strong answer comes down to the same thing: specificity replacing a general claim. The examples below show that shift for two of the frameworks above, with the character count for each version.

History, Question 1 — before (94 characters): "I have always loved History and find the Cold War particularly interesting to study."

History, Question 1 — after (261 characters): "Reading Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers, I was struck by his argument that the First World War resulted from contingent misjudgement rather than inevitable design — a reframing that made me want to study how historians construct causation itself."

Computer Science, Question 2 — before (102 characters): "I have done a lot of independent coding and really enjoy learning new programming languages."

Computer Science, Question 2 — after (256 characters): "Building a scheduling app for my school's clubs, I hit a performance wall once bookings exceeded a few hundred entries — researching this led me to study time-complexity properly, and I rewrote the matching logic using a more efficient algorithm."

In both cases, the "after" version is not simply longer — it replaces an unverifiable claim with a named source, a concrete project, and a specific outcome. That single substitution is the most useful edit any applicant can make to any of the three answers.

Common Mistakes Applicants Are Still Making

Assuming a fourth question exists. It does not, for 2027 entry or any future cycle UCAS has confirmed so far. Three questions only.

Blending Question 2 and Question 3. Academic, qualification-linked preparation belongs in Question 2; everything else belongs in Question 3. Confusing the two weakens both answers.

Writing one essay and splitting it afterwards. Each question should be planned and drafted as its own answer to its own specific wording, then checked together for a consistent narrative — not written as a continuous piece and cut into three.

Ignoring the 350-character minimum. An answer that falls short of 350 characters for any question cannot be submitted, and applicants who leave Question 3 as an afterthought sometimes discover this only at the last moment.

Checking length in a word processor instead of the UCAS form. UCAS counts characters including spaces, and the form's own running counter across all three boxes is the only reliable check.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the UCAS personal statement format changing for 2027 entry?

No. UCAS's guidance covers "2026 entry onwards," and the same three-question format, the same combined 4,000-character limit, and the same 350-character minimum per question carry over unchanged into the 2027 cycle. There is no fourth question and no confirmed plan to alter the structure for 2027 entry.

What are the exact three UCAS personal statement questions?

The three questions, in UCAS's own current wording, are: Question 1, "Why do you want to study this course or subject?"; Question 2, "How have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare for this course or subject?"; and Question 3, "What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful?" Applicants share a combined 4,000-character budget across all three, with a minimum of 350 characters required for each.

What are the key dates for the 2027 UCAS application cycle?

Oxford, Cambridge, and most Medicine, Dentistry and Veterinary Science courses have an earlier equal-consideration deadline of 15 October 2026 at 18:00 UK time. Almost all other undergraduate courses share the main equal-consideration deadline of 13 January 2027 at 18:00 UK time.

What is the difference between Question 2 and Question 3?

Question 2 asks specifically how your "qualifications and studies" have prepared you, so it should stay anchored to formal academic preparation — A-level or IB coursework, an EPQ, subject Olympiads, or directly related wider reading and explain the specific skill each gave you. Question 3 asks what you have done "outside of education," so it covers work experience, volunteering, sport, part-time jobs, and similar activity, provided you explain why each experience is useful for the specific course you are applying to. Blending the two together, which was one of the most common structural mistakes in the first 2026 cycle, weakens both answers.

Since the 2026 cohort became the first to answer these three questions, institutions have started publishing more specific guidance than existed when the format launched.
