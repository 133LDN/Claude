Q1:
Milton Friedman argued in 1953 that an economic theory should be judged by its predictions, not by the realism of its assumptions. I accepted this until I read Amartya Sen's essay "Rational Fools", which argues that the purely economic man is close to being a social moron. Sen's objection unsettled me because Friedman's test cannot catch it: a model of purely selfish agents can predict prices well and still misdescribe people, and that error matters once the same model is used to judge policy. I now think the realism of assumptions matters whenever a model is used to say what people ought to want, not only to forecast what they will do. That question sits between philosophy and economics, which is why I want to study them together. Economics gives me precise tools for asking how incentives shape choices, while philosophy asks whether the preferences we model deserve respect. Reading Michael Sandel on markets crowding out civic norms, I was not convinced by most of his examples, since many are anecdotes. However, the Haifa day-care study he discusses, where a fine for late pick-ups increased lateness, persuaded me that his objection deserves testing rather than dismissal.

Q2:
My Extended Essay asked whether UK minimum wage rises after 2016 reduced youth employment. I expected a clear answer, but the estimates I could reproduce depended on which comparison group I chose, which taught me why econometrics spends so long on identification before it claims anything about causes. IB Philosophy has trained me to reconstruct an argument fairly before attacking it. In a class debate on Rawls I defended the veil of ignorance, but writing up the objection that it assumes extreme risk aversion convinced me that the maximin rule needs more defence than Rawls gives it. Mathematics Analysis and Approaches HL is my hardest subject, and I think it is the most useful, because optimisation is how economists formalise choice and I want to understand a formal model before I criticise it. Theory of Knowledge pushed me on a narrower question: is an economic model a claim about the world or a tool for thinking? My essay argued that it can be both, but only if we state in advance which evidence would count against it, a test I took from Popper and found harder to apply to economics than to physics.

Q3:
Outside school I write a monthly column for our student newsletter that explains one economic idea through a local example, such as why the bus company cut evening routes. Writing for readers who disagree with me taught me to state the strongest version of their view first. I also completed an online course in game theory, and working through repeated prisoner's dilemma problems made me question whether defection is really the rational choice when the game has no fixed end. Working weekends in a café showed me that the owner sets prices by habit rather than marginal cost, which made me more sceptical of textbook pricing models and more curious about behavioural economics.
